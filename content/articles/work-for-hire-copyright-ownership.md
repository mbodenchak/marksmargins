Copyright begins with the author. That seems obvious, but in modern business it gets complicated fast. Companies hire creators, freelancers, designers, and developers. Everyone contributes something that looks like authorship. The question becomes who owns the result. The work-for-hire doctrine answers that question, though not always cleanly.

Under Section 101 of the Copyright Act, a “work made for hire” is either a work created by an employee within the scope of employment, or a specially ordered or commissioned work that fits into one of nine categories, provided there is a written agreement. Those categories include collective works, audiovisual works, translations, and a few other specific forms. Anything outside them does not qualify, no matter what the contract says.

The first category—employee works—is relatively straightforward. If a designer creates a company logo during regular work hours and under supervision, the employer owns the copyright. The employer is considered the legal author from the start. The employee retains no rights unless the company says otherwise. This rule flows from traditional employment law. The company pays for the labor, so it receives the output.

The second category—commissioned works—causes more confusion. Freelancers often sign agreements that claim their work is “made for hire.” But unless the work fits one of those nine statutory categories, that clause is meaningless. The only way ownership transfers is through a separate written assignment. Many people do not realize this distinction until a dispute arises.

One of the most cited cases is Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid. A sculptor was commissioned to create a statue, and the organization claimed it was a work for hire. The Supreme Court disagreed. The sculptor was an independent contractor, not an employee. The organization owned a copy of the sculpture but not the copyright itself. That case shaped how courts now decide who counts as an employee. They use common-law factors like control, supervision, and who provides the tools.

The practical lesson is that contracts must be precise. If a company wants full ownership of commissioned material, it must include both a work-for-hire clause and an explicit assignment of copyright. Without both, the creator might retain the rights even after payment. For paralegals, reviewing or drafting these clauses becomes routine but critical. It is also one of the few moments where form letters and templates can backfire if not updated correctly.

The doctrine also affects joint works, where multiple authors contribute. A joint work is created when two or more people intend their contributions to merge into a single whole. Each author owns an undivided interest. That can create unexpected results. A photographer and a writer collaborating on a magazine piece might each own part of the copyright unless the contract says otherwise.

The simplicity of the rule hides its consequences. Ownership determines who can license, register, or sue for infringement. Getting it wrong can unravel an entire project later. For students, the takeaway is to read Section 101 carefully and always ask, “Who controlled the work, and what did the contract say?” Those two questions decide most ownership disputes.
