Trademarks live long lives, but only if someone keeps them alive. Unlike patents, which expire automatically, trademarks can last forever. Their longevity depends on continued use in commerce and proper maintenance with the United States Patent and Trademark Office.

The process starts with selection. A mark must be distinctive and not likely to cause confusion with an existing registration. Before filing, most practitioners perform a clearance search through the USPTO’s TESS database and often through private search services. That early effort saves months of delay later. Once the mark appears clear, an application is filed either in standard characters or with a design element, depending on whether the client wants word protection or a logo.

Applications may be filed under “use in commerce” or “intent to use.” The difference matters. A use-based application includes specimens showing the mark as actually used on goods or services. An intent-to-use application reserves a spot for a mark that will be used soon. The applicant must later submit a statement of use once sales begin. This flexibility allows companies to secure rights while still developing their products.

After filing, the application enters examination. A USPTO attorney reviews it for compliance with statutory requirements. The most common refusal is under Section 2(d) for likelihood of confusion. The examiner compares the applied-for mark with existing marks and looks at the similarity of goods, services, and trade channels. Other refusals involve descriptiveness, ornamentation, or problems with specimens. Applicants may respond with arguments, amendments, or evidence of acquired distinctiveness. The dialogue can take several rounds before the examiner either approves or issues a final refusal.

If approved, the mark publishes in the Official Gazette for opposition. Any party who believes it would be damaged by registration can oppose within thirty days. Oppositions and cancellations are handled before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, which functions like an administrative court. If no opposition occurs, the mark registers. For intent-to-use filings, registration follows the acceptance of the statement of use.

Once registered, the maintenance period begins. Between the fifth and sixth year after registration, the owner must file a Section 8 declaration of continued use. At the same time, the owner can file a Section 15 declaration of incontestability if the mark has been in continuous use and faces no pending disputes. Every ten years, a combined Section 8 and Section 9 filing renews the registration. For marks obtained through international treaties, Section 71 applies instead. Missing these deadlines leads to cancellation, which wipes out years of brand investment.

Maintenance is not just about paperwork. The owner must also police the mark in the marketplace. Failure to act against unauthorized use can weaken rights or lead to genericide, the process where a brand name becomes the generic term for a product. “Aspirin,” “escalator,” and “cellophane” were once trademarks that died from neglect. Modern companies monitor the internet, trade shows, and advertising for misuse. Many send routine cease-and-desist letters to preserve distinctiveness.

A paralegal working on trademark matters spends much of the time managing calendars and preparing these filings. The work may seem procedural, but it forms the backbone of the entire system. Without timely maintenance, even the most famous mark disappears from the register. The legal principle is simple. Use it or lose it.

Trademarks evolve as living assets. They begin as ideas in marketing meetings, pass through examination, survive opposition, and then grow through consistent use. Decades later, they may become cultural symbols worth billions. Understanding the life cycle from filing to renewal shows how technical precision supports something as intangible as consumer recognition.
